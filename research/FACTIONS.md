# Factions of The Docks

The Docks isn't just a place—it's a ecosystem. Twelve major factions carve up its territories, its profits, and its blood. Here's who they are, where they came from, and what they want.

---

## 1. The Don (The Old Family)

### Origin Story

The Old Family didn't rise—they were planted. In 1974, a young **Salvatore "Sal the Anchor" Moretti** arrived in The Docks with nothing but a fishing boat and a name that meant something back in Naples. He'd fled Italy after a job went wrong in Naples—a collection that became a massacre, and Sal needed to disappear before the Camorra made him a lesson.

The Docks were different then. Brutal, yes, but fragmented. No one owned anything permanently. Sal saw opportunity in chaos. He started small: smuggling Italian goods in with the fish, bribing harbor officials with shares of his profits, building relationships with the working-class Italians who made up half the waterfront labor force. His genius wasn't violence—it was patience. He waited. He watched. He made everyone owe him favors.

By 1982, Sal controlled the import licenses for three major shipping companies. By 1990, he owned the union local. The Old Family wasn't a gang—it was a business with guns, and it had been there longer than anyone could remember.

### Rise to Power

Sal's rise wasn't dramatic. It was surgical. He identified weak points: corrupt officials, desperate boat owners, competing gangsters who bit off more than they could chew. He offered something nobody else did—stability. Do business with The Don, and you'd get paid. Cross The Don, and you'd never work these docks again.

He married his daughters to the families of his key captains, creating a web of loyalty that couldn't be broken by assassination. He kept his hands clean—always had someone else pull triggers—but everyone knew who held the leash. When the federal government cracked down on waterfront organized crime in the 1990s, Sal sacrificed three of his captains to federal prosecutors and walked away untouched. The Old Family adapted. They always did.

### Current Leader

**Don Matteo Moretti** (Sal's grandson, age 54) took over in 2008 after his father Carmine died of a heart attack at 61. Matteo is nothing like his grandfather—where Sal was a shark, Matteo is a python. Patient. Coiled. He strikes only when he's sure to win.

Matteo spent fifteen years learning the family business from the ground up. He worked loading docks, drove trucks, handled books in the family's import legitimate front companies. He's unreadable in meetings, never raises his voice, and has a memory for slights that spans decades. His enemies have a way of disappearing—not violently, but professionally. Careers end. Marriages fall apart. People find themselves audited into poverty.

He's married to a woman from a Genoa shipping family, a marriage of alliances that produced three children—all being groomed for succession. Matteo doesn't trust easily. He trusts even less openly.

### Territory

The Old Family controls the **Old Pier** and **Warehouse Row** along the eastern waterfront—the original heart of The Docks. Their territory includes the shipping terminal gates, the union hall, and the commercial district of Italian restaurants and import shops that cater to the old neighborhood. Their flag is simple: a black anchor on white.

### Resources

- **Legitimate fronts**: Moretti Imports, Atlantic Shipping Co., Harbor View Restaurant Supply
- **Smuggling**: High-end electronics, designer goods, untaxed alcohol, human trafficking (quietly, deny it always)
- **Control points**: Union labor, shipping manifests, dock security contracts
- **Cash flow**: Estimated $40M annually, half legitimate

### Allies

- **The Crown (Rook)**: Old Family money helped build the gambling empire; they share bookkeepers and bank accounts
- **The Ice (Frost)**: Longstanding mutual aid—Frost moves product through Old Family docks
- **The Network (Byte)**: Byte handles the Family's communications security and data protection

### Rivals

- **The Serpents (Viper)**: Tried to move into the Old Family's drug distribution in the 1990s; war ended only when Viper's predecessor agreed to stay out of Old Family territory
- **The Shadows (Shade)**: Constant friction over black market goods; Shade undercut Family prices for years

### Weaknesses

Matteo's greatest weakness is his rigidity—he trusts systems and tradition, and he's slow to adapt to new methods. The Old Family's technology is years behind younger factions. Also, their reliance on legitimate businesses makes them vulnerable to legal pressure—a sustained RICO investigation could crack the family wide open.

### Internal Politics

The Family has a council of five captains who theoretically advise the Don. In practice, Matteo keeps them divided, playing favorites and ensuring no single captain builds enough power to challenge him. His son **Luca** (28) is being positioned for succession, but his daughter **Sofia** (31) is arguably more capable—a power struggle could be brewing.

### Secrets

- The Family maintains a "cleanup fund" used to pay off witnesses, judges, and politicians. It's hidden in a Liechtenstein trust.
- Matteo's father didn't die of natural causes—his heart medication was tampered with, likely by a captain who wanted the top job. Matteo knows but has never acted, waiting for the right moment.
- There are still people alive who can identify the bodies Sal "disposed of" in the 1980s. Byte maintains dirt on them as insurance.

## 2. Viper (The Serpents)

### Origin Story

The Serpents were born in a prison cell. In 1987, three young dealers—**Marcus "Viper" Solomon**, Theresa Huang, and a kid from Baltimore whose name no one remembers—found themselves sharing a cell in state prison. Marcus was in for distribution. Theresa was in for her father's sins. The kid was in over his head.

What they had in common: they'd all been small-time, all been burned by the system, and all understood that the old way—competing individually—was a dead end. While doing their time, they sketched out a plan. When they got out in 1990, they had a brand: The Serpents. A symbol: a coiled snake. And a niche: poison.

Not literal poison—not at first. They specialized in cutting agents, adulterants, and quality control for other dealers' product. If you bought from The Serpents, you knew exactly what you were getting. Their business model was simple: be better than the competition. Cleaner product. Consistent strength. Delivery when promised.

By 1995, they weren't just cutting anymore—they were manufacturing. Theresa's chemistry background (she'd been a pharmacy student before her father's arrest) made them self-sufficient. Marcus's street credibility kept the peace. The kid from Baltimore? He died in a drive-by in 1998, but by then, The Serpents had outgrown needing him.

### Rise to Power

The Serpents' rise came through reputation. While other dealers cut corners, The Serpents invested in quality. Their product was cleaner, their packaging was professional, and their distribution network was tight. They didn't push product onto the streets—they created demand that the streets came to them.

They also pioneered the "franchise" model: independent dealers could operate under The Serpents banner in exchange for a percentage and adherence to quality standards. It was genius. It was also ruthlessly enforced. Dealers who sold substandard product or cheated customers found their operations destroyed—not by violence, but by The Serpents simply refusing to supply them.

By 2005, The Serpents controlled 60% of the powder cocaine and methamphetamine distribution in The Docks. By 2015, they'd expanded into synthetic opioids and were experimenting with designer drugs. They were the backbone of the local drug trade.

### Current Leader

**Theresa "Viper" Huang** (born 1968) has led The Serpents since Marcus was killed in 2011. She never wanted the top job—she wanted to be left alone in a lab. But Marcus made her his successor anyway, trusting her judgment more than anyone else's.

Theresa is 56 years old, five-foot-two, with graying hair she keeps in a practical ponytail and hands that still tremble slightly from a mild neurotoxin exposure in 2009. She's not a fighter. She's not charismatic. She's brilliant at chemistry and ruthless in business. Her meetings are short, her questions are pointed, and she has zero tolerance for waste or incompetence.

She has no family—her father was a crime lord who died in prison, her mother slit her wrists when Theresa was sixteen. She lives alone in a fortified apartment above one of her labs, surrounded by books and lab equipment. Her only indulgence is expensive green tea.

### Territory

The Serpents control **Chemical Row** and the **Southside Storage Units**, where their labs and distribution centers are hidden in plain sight. They also maintain a presence in the **Lower Harbor** area, where product arrives via boat. Their territory is marked by a subtle snake tattoo that appears on walls and dumpsters—more warning than advertisement.

### Resources

- **Labs**: Three hidden production facilities producing cocaine cut, methamphetamine, synthetic opioids, and experimental designer drugs
- **Distribution**: Franchise network of 40+ independent dealers across The Docks
- **Chemical supply**: Relationships with corrupt chemical suppliers and international synthesis networks
- **Quality control**: Professional packaging, consistent dosing, GPS-tracked deliveries
- **Cash flow**: Estimated $25M annually (all illicit)

### Allies

- **The Embers (Blaze)**: Tactical alliance—Blaze supplies precursor chemicals; they share distribution networks and occasionally cooperate on production
- **The Shadows (Shade)**: Shade provides cover operations (front businesses, storage) in exchange for product
- **The Jackals (Fang)**: When Fang needs pharmaceutical-grade supplies for their medical experimentation, The Serpents provide

### Rivals

- **The Old Family (The Don)**: Old dispute over territory; The Serpents were pushed out of the eastern docks in the 1990s and want it back
- **The Iron Hands**: Competition over vehicle logistics for distribution—Iron won't let Serpents use their chop shops for mobile labs

### Weaknesses

Theresa's isolation is a liability—she doesn't have deep relationships with her dealers, and there's no clear succession plan. Her labs are vulnerable to raids (they've lost two in the last five years to federal sting operations). Also, her dependence on Blaze for precursors gives that faction leverage.

### Internal Politics

The Serpents have a clear hierarchy—Theresa at the top, then three "Head Dealers" who manage the franchise network, then the franchisees themselves. But there are tensions. Some younger members think Theresa is too conservative; they want to push into new markets (fentanyl analogs, distribution in other cities). Theresa has resisted, arguing that growth invites attention. A faction led by **Jared "Sidewinder" Cole** (32) is increasingly vocal about expansion.

### Secrets

- Theresa has a private lab where she's working on a "next-generation" opioid—something that could revolutionize (and devastate) the industry. It's untested on humans.
- The original Marcus Solomon isn't dead—he's in federal witness protection, and Theresa pays his silence. She checks in on him annually.
- Three of their franchisees are actually federal informants. Theresa knows about two of them.

## 3. Rook (The Crown)

### Origin Story

The Crown didn't start with cards—it started with a debt. In 1996, **Raymond "Rook" Castellano** was a mid-level enforcer for The Old Family, working off a gambling debt to Sal Moretti's son Carmine. Raymond was good at his job: he broke knees, collected money, and never asked questions. But he was also smarter than his employers realized.

While working collections, Raymond noticed something: the illegal poker games scattered across The Docks were generating enormous cash flow, but they were all disorganized. No统一的规则, no protection for players, no consistency. The games got robbed constantly. Players got cheated. The whole thing was inefficient.

Raymond had an idea. He approached Carmine with a proposition: let him organize the games, take a percentage, and the Family would get their debt paid off plus a steady cut. Carmine agreed, thinking he'd be getting a loyal enforcer. Instead, he was building his own competition.

Within two years, Raymond controlled twelve poker games across The Docks. Within five, he controlled them all—and he'd started adding craps, blackjack, and sports betting. By 2003, he didn't need The Old Family anymore. He paid off his debt in full, told Carmine to go to hell, and founded The Crown.

### Rise to Power

The Crown's power came from organization. Where other gambling operations were chaos, Rook brought rules. Every game had security. Every bet was protected. Every player knew exactly what they were getting. He created a reputation: if you played in a Crown game, you'd get paid. If you tried to cheat, you'd lose fingers. Simple.

He also understood leverage. He kept detailed files on every player—winning streaks, losing streaks, debts, secrets. He knew who could be blackmailed, who could be turned, and who would never pay. His information network was as valuable as his games.

By 2010, The Crown had expanded beyond gambling into loan sharking, bail bonds, and "protection" for legitimate businesses. They weren't just running games—they were running a financial empire. And they were untouchable, because they kept everything in cash, in people's heads, and in books that never existed.

### Current Leader

**Raymond "The Rook" Castellano** (born 1969) is 55 years old, silver-haired, and built like a man who used to fight but now pays others to do it for him. He has a face that's hard to read—broad jaw, deep-set eyes, a mouth that never quite smiles. He dresses well (always a suit, always expensive) and speaks softly. When he raises his voice, people listen. When he goes quiet, people worry.

Raymond is a chess player—he thinks three moves ahead, always. He's patient, calculating, and has an eidetic memory for numbers and faces. He's married twice (both ended badly) and has a son (26) who works as a corporate lawyer in another city—a deliberate distance from the family business.

His reputation is built on fairness (within the context of crime) and ruthlessness when crossed. He killed his own cousin in 2008 for cheating at cards. He considered it unfortunate but necessary.

### Territory

The Crown controls **Cardinal Square** (the central district of The Docks, named for the cardinal directions), where their high-stakes games operate in private backrooms above legitimate businesses. They also maintain the **Betting Blocks**—residential buildings where sports betting and numbers games run around the clock. Their territory is marked by a subtle crown symbol scratched into doorframes.

### Resources

- **Games**: High-stakes poker, craps, blackjack in seven private locations
- **Betting**: Sports betting, horse racing, numbers racket, prop bets
- **Loans**: Private lending with usurious interest rates (5-10% weekly)
- **Protection**: "Insurance" payments from 30+ businesses across The Docks
- **Cash flow**: Estimated $30M annually (mix of legitimate and illicit)

### Allies

- **The Old Family (The Don)**: Old alliance, rekindled after Rook paid his debt; they share financial infrastructure and banking relationships
- **The Ice (Frost)**: Mutual betting interests—Frost runs sports betting through Crown networks in winter months
- **The Ravens (Ghost)**: Ghost provides intelligence on high rollers and potential marks

### Rivals

- **The Jackals (Fang)**: Fang has tried to muscle in on loan-sharking territory; several violent confrontations
- **The Drift (Storm)**: Storm's nomadic crews occasionally rob Crown betting operations; it's an ongoing low-grade conflict

### Weaknesses

The Crown's reliance on cash makes them vulnerable to counterfeit operations—they've been burned twice in the last three years. Also, their business model depends on trust, and trust can be betrayed; there's always someone trying to run their own game outside the network. The federal government has been circling for years—a major RICO case is an ever-present threat.

### Internal Politics

Raymond has no clear successor. His son wants nothing to do with the business. His three top lieutenants—**Eddie "Bishop" Marchetti**, **Diane "Knight" Okonkwo**, and **Tommy "Pawn" Varga**—all have ambitions. They've been jockeying for position for years, and the tension is starting to show. Raymond knows and encourages it (divide and conquer), but he's 55 and doesn't have forever.

### Secrets

- Raymond has been quietly buying legitimate businesses as a "retirement plan"—if the bottom falls out, he can disappear into a new identity as a hotel owner
- He keeps a private file on every politician, cop, and judge in The Docks. It's encrypted and stored on a server controlled by Byte.
- His first wife didn't leave him—she was buried in the foundation of a building the Crown renovated in 2004. He still owns the building.

## 4. Ghost (The Ravens)

### Origin Story

The Ravens started as a joke. In 2002, four hackers and one ex-cop met in an online forum dedicated to "information liberation." Their usernames were all bird names. The ex-cop's was "Ghost." They were bored, smart, and angry at a world that had chewed them up.

The ex-cop was **James Whitmore**, a former homicide detective who'd been forced off the force in 2000 after exposing a corruption scandal that implicated his own captain. He didn't expose it fast enough to save his career, but he exposed it thoroughly enough to make enemies. He needed to disappear.

The hackers were different—they were young, digital-native, and believed information wanted to be free. But they also understood that information had value, and that value could be monetized. They started small: background checks, surveillance sweeps, corporate espionage. By 2005, they'd learned to monetize their skills more directly: blackmail, identity theft, and—most profitable—assassination contracts.

The name "Ghost" was James's call sign from his cop days. "The Ravens" was the group's private joke—a reference to Poe, because they all thought they were clever. The name stuck. By 2008, they weren't a joke anymore.

### Rise to Power

The Ravens' rise was invisible—and that's the point. They never held territory. They never controlled product. They controlled information, and information was more valuable than either.

Their breakthrough came in 2010, when they were contracted by a local crime lord to dig up dirt on a rival. They found not just dirt but a body—the rival had killed a federal witness in 2008 and buried him in a concrete foundation. The client used the information to destroy the rival's organization. The Ravens learned something valuable: the truth was a weapon, and they were ammo specialists.

After that, contracts poured in. They provided intelligence to factions who needed to know who was talking to the FBI. They blackmailed politicians, corrupt cops, and businessmen. And when the money was right, they killed—quietly, cleanly, with no evidence left behind.

By 2015, every major faction in The Docks was either a client or a target. The Ravens didn't need to hold territory because they were everywhere.

### Current Leader

**James "Ghost" Whitmore** (born 1971) is 53 years old and looks like a man who's been tired for twenty years. He's lean, gray-bearded, with eyes that move constantly—he's always scanning, always processing. He wears glasses (he claims 20/20 vision; the glasses are psychological) and dresses in boring clothes that make him forgettable. Good.

James was a detective for twelve years. He's a perfectionist, a control freak, and deeply paranoid—the last one has kept him alive. He doesn't trust easily, and his trust is always conditional. He has no family (ex-wife, no kids), no hobbies, no life outside The Ravens. His work is his identity.

His leadership style is hands-on—he's still the one running surveillance on high-value targets, still the one personally reviewing sensitive contracts. He kills personally when the target warrants it (and he considers it a compliment when he does).

### Territory

The Ravens have no fixed territory. They're distributed across The Docks, operating from safe houses, rented rooms, and coffee shops. Their "headquarters" is a rotating set of encrypted servers scattered across the city. They have a physical base—a reinforced basement in the **Old Harbor** district—but only three people know its location.

### Resources

- **Intelligence**: Surveillance, background checks, wiretaps, hacking, data mining
- **Assassination**: Clean kills, staged accidents, poisoned drinks, remote eliminations
- **Blackmail**: Compiled dossiers on politicians, cops, businessmen, and other criminals
- **Counter-intelligence**: Sweeps for bugs, GPS trackers, and surveillance
- **Cash flow**: Estimated $15M annually (all from services)

### Allies

- **The Crown (Rook)**: Longstanding contract—Raven provides intelligence on high rollers and players who might be trouble
- **The Shadows (Shade)**: Shade feeds them tips on valuable targets in exchange for favors
- **Every faction, technically**: The Ravens will work for anyone who can pay, with very few exceptions

### Rivals

- **The Network (Byte)**: Competing information services; there's an uneasy détente, but they both know it's temporary
- **The Iron Hands**: Iron tried to strong-arm The Ravens into free work; they responded by releasing damning evidence about Iron's leadership to federal contacts

### Weaknesses

The Ravens are small—they have maybe fifteen active members, and only five are true operators. They're also dependent on trust, and trust is fragile; a single traitor could expose everything. Their work depends on digital security, and digital security can always be breached. They have no muscle of their own—they have to hire freelancers for physical work, which introduces variables.

### Internal Politics

The Ravens have a flat structure: James at the top, then a circle of "operators" who have equal voice in meetings. But there are tensions. **Maya "Raven" Chen** (34), the best hacker in the group, has been pushing for more autonomy—she wants to run her own contracts without James's approval. James has been resisting, but she's getting impatient. A split could be coming.

### Secrets

- James keeps a backup of everything—every contract, every piece of blackmail, every secret—in a safety deposit box in another city. It's insurance.
- One of their "associates" is actually an FBI informant. James knows, but hasn't moved against them yet—useful to feed controlled information.
- They maintain a private eye in every major faction's inner circle—someone who doesn't know they're feeding information to The Ravens.

## 5. Iron (The Iron Hands)

### Origin Story

The Iron Hands were forged in blood and grease. In 1991, **Viktor Kowalski** was a mechanic at a body shop in the industrial district—a Polish immigrant who worked hard, kept his head down, and tried to build a life. He was good with cars, good with engines, and good with his hands. He was also, as it turned out, good with violence.

In 1993, Viktor witnessed a murder. Two members of a drug crew had killed a rival in the parking lot behind his shop. They saw him watching. Instead of killing him, they made him an offer: they'd let him live if he agreed to modify their cars—remove vin numbers, install hidden compartments, make them "disappear."

Viktor agreed. For two years, he did their work. He learned the criminal side of the auto business: chop shops, VIN switching, smuggling goods in vehicle hidden compartments. And he learned something else: these guys were amateurs. Their security was sloppy. Their cars were shit. Their operation could be taken over.

In 1995, Viktor killed his "employers" in their sleep, took over their network, and founded The Iron Hands. He was twenty-seven years old, and he'd just become the most important man in The Docks for anyone who needed wheels.

### Rise to Power

The Iron Hands' power is simple: they control vehicle logistics. Every faction needs cars—running product, fleeing crime scenes, transporting people. And every faction needs those cars modified: hidden compartments, armor, tracking detection, emergency equipment. The Iron Hands do it all, and they do it better than anyone.

Viktor's genius was monopolization. He didn't just compete with other chop shops—he destroyed them. He'd find a competitor, learn their weaknesses, and either hire their best people or burn their operation to the ground. By 2005, there were no other chop shops in The Docks. There was only Iron.

But Viktor also understood loyalty. He never overcharged his regular clients. He gave good rates to factions that didn't compete with his interests. He built relationships—real ones, with people who trusted him. When other factions needed emergency vehicles (a raid gone wrong, a fast escape), Iron delivered. They became essential.

### Current Leader

**Viktor "Iron" Kowalski** (born 1968) is 56 years old, barrel-chested, with hands like shovels and a face that's all scar tissue on one side. He lost half his face in a car bomb in 2008—an attempt on his life by a rival faction that failed only because Viktor had installed custom armor in his vehicle that morning.

Viktor is old-school. He works six days a week in his main garage, getting his hands dirty, because he doesn't trust anyone else to do the job right. He's gruff, foul-mouthed, and has no patience for weakness. But he's fair—he pays well, protects his people, and never stiffs a client.

He was married once, briefly, in the 1990s. It ended badly (she took exception to his lifestyle). He has a daughter (28) who works as a mechanic in another city and wants nothing to do with him. He has a son (22) who does work for the organization—and who is, in Viktor's own words, "not ready for what's coming."

### Territory

The Iron Hands control **Iron Works**—the industrial district in the western Docks, a collection of garages, warehouses, and body shops that has been their home for twenty-five years. Their main facility is a massive warehouse that used to be a shipyard. It's fortified, surveilled, and defended by a dozen loyalists. The symbol of Iron Works is a metal fist painted on every door.

### Resources

- **Chop shops**: Three facilities for disassembling stolen vehicles, stripping parts, and reassembling for resale
- **Modifications**: Custom hidden compartments, armor, tracking detection, engine upgrades, emergency equipment
- **Vehicle supply**: Stolen cars, imported through networks, sold to factions and private buyers
- **Fleet management**: Maintain and repair the vehicles of six different factions
- **Cash flow**: Estimated $20M annually (mix of legitimate and illicit)

### Allies

- **The Embers (Blaze)**: Longstanding relationship—Blaze uses Iron's vehicles for their runs, and Iron gets preferred pricing on fuel and chemicals
- **The Drift (Storm)**: Storm is essentially a mobile force that depends on Iron for vehicles; they're practically joined at the hip
- **The Ice (Frost)**: Mutual aid—Frost needs cold storage trucks, Iron modifies them

### Rivals

- **The Serpents (Viper)**: Ongoing conflict over mobile labs—Viper wants to use Iron's vehicles for drug production, Viktor refuses (too much heat)
- **The Jackals (Fang)**: Fang has tried to steal Iron's vehicle supply chain; two violent confrontations in the last three years

### Weaknesses

Iron is traditional—and traditional can mean slow to adapt. Viktor resists new technology, which puts them behind factions like Byte. Also, their operation is physically visible (garages, warehouses) which makes them a target for law enforcement. They've been raided three times in ten years—they always reopen, but it's expensive.

### Internal Politics

Viktor's son **Mikhail** (22) is being groomed for leadership, but he's not ready. He lacks his father's instinct and has a tendency to trust the wrong people. There's a lieutenant—**Frank "Wrench" Morales** (45)—who's been with Viktor for twenty years and who arguably has more real authority than Mikhail does. If Viktor dies or retires, there's going to be a succession crisis.

### Secrets

- Viktor keeps a garage hidden in a private location with four vehicles prepped for "emergency extraction"—his escape plan if things go wrong
- They've been quietly installing tracking devices in vehicles they sell to rival factions—not for control, but for insurance
- Their legitimate body shop is a front for money laundering, but the books are meticulously maintained—Viktor is obsessive about keeping the legal side clean

## 6. Fang (The Jackals)

### Origin Story

The Jackals started in a graveyard. Literally. In 2003, a group of scavengers began raiding the abandoned warehouses and industrial sites in the southern Docks—the areas too contaminated, too dangerous, or too forgotten for anyone else to bother with. They found value in what others threw away: scrap metal, copper wiring, abandoned equipment, forgotten cargo in rotting ships.

The leader was a woman named **Isabel "Fang" Medina**, a former paramedic who'd been blacklisted from every hospital in the city after a patient died under her care (it wasn't her fault, but the lawsuit killed her career). She was desperate, skilled, and angry. She gathered other outcasts—ex-workers, ex-convicts, people with nothing to lose—and built something from nothing.

They weren't just scavengers—they were finders. They found things others lost: bodies, evidence, missing shipments, hidden stashes. They developed a reputation: if you needed something found, The Jackals could find it. For a price.

By 2008, they'd expanded into "collection"—recovering debts that others had given up on, extracting information from reluctant sources, and providing "extraction services" for anyone who could pay. They weren't killers, exactly—but they weren't not killers either.

### Rise to Power

The Jackals' rise came from specialization. Everyone else in The Docks was focused on their core business—product, territory, money. The Jackals focused on what was left over: the scraps, the leftovers, the things nobody wanted to handle. And they found that the scraps were valuable.

They also found that information was everywhere—if you knew where to look. Their scavenger network gave them eyes and ears across the entire Docks, in places no one else bothered to watch. They knew about deals before they happened, about conflicts before they started, about secrets that had been buried for years. They became the faction you went to when you needed to know something nobody else knew.

By 2015, The Jackals weren't just scavengers—they were researchers, investigators, and (when the price was right) interrogators. They had information on everyone, and they knew how to use it.

### Current Leader

**Isabel "Fang" Medina** (born 1975) is 49 years old, sharp-featured, with black hair that's usually pulled back and eyes that miss nothing. She's not tall, not imposing, not physically threatening—but she has a presence that makes people uncomfortable. She looks at you like she's taking inventory.

Isabel is a researcher at heart. She loves data, information, patterns. She can spend hours poring over old records, trying to reconstruct the history of a shipment or a person. She's methodical, patient, and ruthless when she needs to be. She views the world as a puzzle to be solved.

She has no family (estranged from parents who never understood her), no partner (there's a woman in her life, but it's complicated), and no life outside The Jackals. She lives in a cramped apartment above a thrift store, surrounded by books, files, and computers. Her only vice is expensive coffee.

### Territory

The Jackals control the **Scrapyards**—the southernmost district of The Docks, a collection of salvage yards, abandoned warehouses, and trash-strewn lots that stretch to the city's edge. They also maintain a network of "collection points" throughout The Docks, hidden locations where recovered goods are stored and sorted. Their territory is marked by scratched jackal heads on poles and fences.

### Resources

- **Scrap operations**: Three salvage yards processing metal, electronics, and equipment
- **Search and recovery**: Finding missing items, people, or information for paying clients
- **Debt collection**: Aggressive recovery of bad debts, with "persuasion" if necessary
- **Information network**: Eyes and ears across the entire Docks, fed by scavenger teams
- **Cash flow**: Estimated $12M annually (mix of legitimate salvage and services)

### Allies

- **The Shadows (Shade)**: Shade uses Jackals to locate "lost" goods and provide intelligence on competitors
- **The Ravens (Ghost)**: Mutual information exchange—Ghost feeds Jackals tips on valuable targets, Jackals provides physical surveillance
- **The Network (Byte)**: Byte uses Jackals to physically retrieve data from locations that can't be hacked

### Rivals

- **The Crown (Rook)**: Rook considers Jackal debt collection to be encroaching on his territory; tensions are ongoing
- **The Iron Hands (Iron)**: Conflict over scrap metal—Jackals sometimes "find" Iron's stolen parts, which Iron considers theft
- **The Drift (Storm)**: Storm occasionally raids Jackal territory for supplies; it's an ongoing annoyance

### Weaknesses

The Jackals are stretched thin—they cover a lot of ground with limited people. Their information network is valuable but diffuse; it's hard to turn information into actionable intelligence quickly. Also, their territory is poor—the scrapyards don't generate the money that drug or gambling territories do. They're always cash-poor.

### Internal Politics

Isabel is the uncontested leader, but she has a problem: her second-in-command, **Marcus "Scar" Webb** (42), is increasingly ambitious. He thinks The Jackals should be more aggressive, should take more territory, should go after the big money. Isabel thinks they should stay small, stay hidden, stay valuable. The tension is growing.

### Secrets

- Isabel maintains a private database of every death in The Docks over the last twenty years—not just the known ones, but the suspected ones, the covered-up ones. It's blackmail insurance.

## 7. Storm (The Drift)

## 8. Shade (The Shadows)

By 2015, The Shadows were the connective tissue of The Docks. Every faction used them for something. They didn't hold territory because they didn't need to—they held relationships, and relationships were more valuable than street corners.

## 9. Blaze (The Embers)
